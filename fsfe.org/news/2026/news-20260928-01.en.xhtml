Software Freedom in Europe 2026

In 2026 we turned 25 years old! We continued enforcing and contributing to the development of DMA and followed up the technological sovereignty debates across Europe explaining why only Free Software gives public institutions control over the digital infrastructure. Ada & Zangemann and YH4F continued bringing software freedom to younger audiences.

This year marks a significant milestone for the FSFE: our 25th anniversary. What began in 2001 as a small organisation founded by a small group of European Free Software advocates to protect Free Software in Europe has grown into a Europe-wide, well-known organisation with hundreds of volunteers, thousands of supporters, and impact across several key areas. Over the past quarter-century, we have focused our work on public awareness, policy, and legal education to fulfil the mission our founders set out. One that is even more important today than ever.

This anniversary is more than a celebration of longevity; it is an opportunity to reflect on the journey that brought us here and on our work over these years. We have summarised our key achievements from the past 25 years in our timeline.

As we look back on 25 years of Software Freedom, we also look forward. The following report highlights the milestones of the past year, work that builds on everything we have accomplished before, while also looking ahead to our vision for 2048.

Our Software Freedom in Europe 2025 report covers the FSFE's activities from September 2025 to August 2026. We hope it gives you a better understanding of our daily work and that you enjoy reading it!

Device Neutrality

DMA Litigation and beyond

The FSFE continued working to ensure that the Digital Markets Act (DMA) translates into meaningful interoperability and Device Neutrality for Free Software developers and users. Over the year, we combined technical research, regulatory engagement and strategic litigation, with a particular focus on Apple and Google.

A major milestone came on 21 October 2025, when the FSFE participated as an intervener in the hearing of Apple v Commission (T-1080/23) before the European Court of Justice. We defended interoperability as a legitimate public-interest obligation and brought the perspective of Free Software developers and users into Apple’s challenge to its DMA designation and obligations. In July 2026, the Court dismissed Apple’s actions concerning the App Store and iOS designation; importantly, it held that Apple could not use the designation proceedings to challenge Article 6(7), without ruling on the substance of that provision. The Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) subsequently recognised FSFE’s contribution to the proceedings.

On 20 April 2026, we published a report into Apple’s request-based interoperability process. We found that, as of 22 March, none of 56 formal interoperability requests had resulted in a new publicly documented solution. Developers faced account requirements, fees, lengthy procedures and considerable gatekeeper discretion. We called for transparent procedures, stronger enforcement and greater reliance on open standards, while continuing to collect first-hand experiences from developers through our interoperability survey.

In May 2026, the FSFE was admitted as an intervener in a second case, Apple v Commission (T-359/25). This case goes directly to the practical implementation of Article 6(7), as Apple challenges the Commission decision specifying procedures for access to software and hardware features. Significantly, the Court recognised when admitting us that the outcome could have a substantial impact on the supply of Free and Open Source Software and developers’ ability to interconnect their applications with Apple’s operating systems. Specialised media, including TPP, covered our admission to the second Apple case, highlighting the growing role of civil society in DMA enforcement and the importance of the case for Free Software developers’ ability to build and distribute interoperable alternatives.

Our DMA work also increasingly focused on Google and Android. On 26-27 May 2026, at the SCiDA Conference in Düsseldorf, we presented our technical research into Android custom ROMs, device trees and commit histories, bringing evidence from Free Software development into discussions on digital competition enforcement. On 15 June, we contributed to the European Commission’s Android interoperability proceedings, calling the full uninstallation of AI-based features to be possible and arguing that access to interoperability should not depend on Google’s developer-verification requirements. In our view, interoperability is not genuinely independent if developers must first register with, identify themselves to, or enter into a contractual relationship with the gatekeeper.

We also contributed to the broader development of DMA enforcement. Together with civil-society partners, we participated in the Commission’s first formal evaluation of the DMA, advocating stronger Device Neutrality, effective side-loading and interoperability rules that work not only for large companies, but also for community projects, non-profits and independent developers. Our research also extended beyond individual DMA proceedings. In 2026, our colleague Lucas Lasota, who also works for the Martin-Luther University of Halle-Wittenberg published a comparative study of interoperability regulation in the EU, Japan, the UK and Brazil, examining how such rules can give Free Software access to features controlled by dominant proprietary platforms and enable smaller developers and communities to compete. We also were guests at renowned content producers to talk about our advocacy in regulatory procedures involving Apple.

Throughout the year, we brought these experiences into the wider policy debate: at the ARTICLE 19 DMA Enforcement Symposium, CPDP 2026, the UCL/KCL conference on private enforcement, and through articles in specialised media examining developer-friendly DMA enforcement and the changing governance of Android. We also discussed our work through the SCiDA Podcast and, in August 2026, the Software Freedom Podcast on Keeping Android Open campaing.

Across these activities, our focus remained consistent: interoperability must work in practice, not only on paper. For the DMA to strengthen user choice and competition, Free Software communities, independent developers and users must be able to access platform features and install and run software of their choice without unnecessary gatekeeper control.

Router Freedom faces another reform at the EU level

In 2026, our Router Freedom work shifted increasingly to the European level. With the European Commission's proposed Digital Networks Act (DNA) now on the table, the FSFE saw an opportunity to finally close the fragmentation gap that has weakened Router Freedom since the last telecoms reform, when national regulators were left free to exclude this right for certain network topologies.

In February 2026, the FSFE called on the EU to establish strong and consistent protection of Router Freedom in the DNA. Whether users can actually exercise their right to choose their own router still depends on where the Network Termination Point (NTP) is defined: if it sits behind a provider-supplied router, Router Freedom exists on paper but not in practice. Pointing to continued fragmentation, we submitted our position to the European Commission's consultation on the DNA, calling for amendments to the text that would anchor the NTP at the passive physical point of the network by default across all Member States.

Looking forward, the FSFE is looking at how satellite internet providers are handling Router Freedom, and what are the challenges under EU legislation.

A decade-long victory for Radio Equipment Directive

Two months later came a second, decade-in-the-making success. In April 2026, we announced victory in preventing "Radio Lockdown": the European Commission decided to abandon Article 3(3)(i) of the Radio Equipment Directive, a provision that, since 2014, had threatened to force manufacturers to block users from installing their own software on WiFi routers, phones, and other radio devices. The Commission's decision followed an impact assessment that found the risks it was meant to address remained theoretical, and it explicitly cited the legal study the FSFE commissioned from Dr. Till Jaeger showing the provision was incompatible with licences like the GNU GPL. It is a strong reminder that patient, evidence-based engagement with EU policy processes pays off, even when it takes more than ten years.

Want to hear the full story behind this decade-long fight? Check out the episode 45 of the Software Freedom Podcast. There we sit down with our former colleague Max Mehl who first flagged the issue back in 2015 and trace together the Radio Equipment Directive timeline, from a printed-out draft directive to its final chapter.

Legal Education

Next Generation Internet (NGI)

Since 2018, the FSFE has been a consortium member of the Next Generation Internet Zero (NGI0) initiative, supported by the European Commission. The mission of the NGI has been to provide support to various free and open data, hardware, and software projcets across Europe, in order to re-imagine and re-engineer the internet into a value oriented, human-centric, and inclusive platform that can yield a net benefit for society.

Over the past 8 years, the FSFE has been providing guidance to successful grantee software projects on legal and licensing issues, in addition to helping them become REUSE compliant. Through individual assessments and direct assistance, we aim to promote the display of unambiguous, human-readable, and machine-readable licence and copyright information. Other consortium partners provide support on issues that can improve each grantee software project, such as security, accessibility, and translations, among others.

Unfortunately, our work under the NGI umbrella will be winding down, as funding for the vast NGI project ecosystem has been cut. This has in turn led to funding projects such as NGI to no longer be part of future Horizon Europe funding drafts and work programmes. The FSFE is currently working on 2 separate NGI0 projects, with both expecting to conclude by mid-2027.

Nevertheless, while one door closes on the NGI0 projects, another opens. Together with familiar consortium partners that FSFE has been working with over the past 8 years, the FSFE had been searching for an alternative to provide similar support for Free Software projects in Europe. We are happy that our efforts to do so can continue, as we will be part of 2 newly approved Horizon Europe projects: “Restack” and “GeoCommons”.

Europe’s need for control over its own digital infrastructure has grown more urgent. The “cloud-first” strategy that has dominated in the past decade has shown itself to be an unsustainable long-term strategy in light of geopolitical stressors. Under Restack , the FSFE will support software projects in Europe that help to strengthen the Free Software ecosystem, improve the resilience of the internet, improve transparency in digital technology, and promote European digital sovereignty. Similarly under GeoCommons, the FSFE will work to support free and open geospatial data, standards, and tools.

Markets Regulations

Shaping the implementation of the Cyber Resilience Act

The implementation of the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) remained a key focus of our work on Free Software Markets. Building on our first stakeholder survey on the role of Free Software in the CRA, we developed recommendations based on the experiences and concerns of Free Software projects, potential stewards and manufacturers. We brought these findings into our exchanges with the European Commission and market surveillance authorities, helping to shape the Commission’s practical guidance for implementing the CRA.

As the implementation process moved forward, we turned our attention to remaining questions, in particular the role of Article 25 of the CRA. At FOSDEM, the FSFE discussed these issues together with representatives of the European Commission and the German market surveillance authority. Our Software Freedom Podcast took a closer look at stewards, security attestations and the CRA implementation process.

Article 25 foresees voluntary security attestation programmes for Free Software, which will be specified in a delegated act. We launched a new survey specifically dedicated to Article 25, incorporating input from individual developers, Free Software projects, manufacturers and other stakeholders. The survey results were presented at FrOSCon, where we discussed how they can feed into the upcoming delegated act. Going forward, we will continue to advocate for an appropriate framework that does not place disproportionate burdens on individual Free Software developers and small projects.

Public Money? Public Code!

Public Money? Public Code! in the EU

Technological sovereignty has been at the centre of debates across Europe this year. The European Commission's Tech Sovereignty Package (TSP) contains a long-overdue revision of the EU Open Source Strategy and the proposal for the Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA), which introduces a provision promoting Free Software solutions for public cloud and AI procurement. But despite the TSP citing our “Public Money? Public Code!” demand, several concerns remain, and the FSFE will continue working with the co-legislators to push for a pragmatic, strong, and transparent Free Software first provision.

The year 2026 marks two years since the adoption of the Interoperable Europe Act (IEA). We have continued engaging with members of the Interoperable Europe Board to make sure its implementation is underpinned by the principle of “Public Money? Public Code!” Under the new IEA umbrella, the European Interoperability Framework is undergoing its first update in nine years. The FSFE is calling for Free Software and Open Standards to be the default model for digital public services and for the development of procurement guidance to prevent openwashing.

The upcoming revision of EU procurement rules will shape software procurement in Europe for years to come. The FSFE contributed to the European Commission’s consultation, calling for strategic procurement rules that enable public administrations to choose, adapt, and jointly develop Free Software. We also warned that “made in Europe” criteria alone do not foster digital sovereignty. Only Free Software gives public institutions control over the digital infrastructure they rely on. In addition, we call for effective safeguards against vendor lock-in and openwashing in public procurement.

Digital sovereignty requires Free Software

Over the past year, the FSFE has continued its work to make Free Software the foundation of digital sovereignty. As European governments are increasingly looking for alternatives to their dependence on large non-European technology companies, we have warned that “buying European” alone does not solve the sovereignty problem. European proprietary software creates new dependencies and vendor lock-in. In our feedback to the German government’s consultation on the “Germany Stack”, we therefore called for the platform to be developed entirely as Free Software, aligned with similar efforts across Europe and backed by secure, long-term funding.

Showcasing best practices and challenging setbacks

Our watchdog activities remain an important part of “Public Money? Public Code!”, as progress towards public code is not linear. When reports emerged that NHS England intended to make most of its public source-code repositories private by default, the FSFE publicly challenged the plan. Removing source code from public view is not an effective security measure. Instead, it prevents independent scrutiny, reuse, and collaborative improvement. Although NHS England has not reversed its course as of now, the FSFE sent a clear public signal that publicly funded code must remain public and will continue to follow the issue. We have also continued criticising openwashing and raising awareness about misleading practices and how they can be tackled.

Alongside our policy and watchdog work, we continued showcasing practical experiences with Free Software in public administrations. In a Software Freedom Podcast episode, we presented X-Road as an example of shared Free Software infrastructure and discussed some of the challenges such projects face in public institutions.

In the last twelve months, we want to especially thank our donors who contributed 18,000 € or more per year: Qualtom, Proton Pass, and Linuxhotel. At the same time, we want to express our heartfelt gratitude to everyone making smaller contributions. Even if the amount looks modest, we know it can be a real effort, and together these donations make a big difference in sustaining our work.

We guarantee that all support is used to foster software freedom in Europe a little more each day, step by step, bit by bit, for the next decades.

With your help we can keep on defending software freedom. Thank you for your trust, your support, and your ideas to continue bringing Free Software to our society!
